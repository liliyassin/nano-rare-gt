Focused ultrasound and microbubble-mediated
delivery of CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein
to human induced pluripotent stem cells
Kyle Hazel,1 Davindra Singh,1 Stephanie He,1 Zakary Guertin,1 Mathieu C. Husser, 1 and Brandon Hel ﬁeld1,2
1Department of Biology, Concordia University, 7141 Sherbrooke St. W, H4B 1R6 Montreal, QC, Canada; 2Department of Physics, Concordia University, 7141 Sherbrooke

CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) have been heavily
considered for gene therapy due to their high on-target ef ﬁ-
ciency, rapid activity, and lack of insertional mutagenesis rela-
tive to other CRISPR-Cas9 delivery formats. Genetic diseases
such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy currently lack effective
treatment strategies and are prime targets for CRISPR-Cas9
gene editing technology. However, current in vivo delivery stra-
tegies for Cas9 pose risks of unwanted immunogenic responses.
This proof-of-concept study aimed to demonstrate that focused
ultrasound (FUS) in combination with microbubbles can be
used to deliver Cas9-sgRNA (single-guide RNA) RNPs and
functionally edit human induced pluripotent stem cells
(hiPSCs) in vitro, a model system that can be expanded to car-
diovascular research via hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes. Here,
we ﬁrst determine acoustic conditions suitable for the viable
delivery of large proteins to hiPSCs with clinical De ﬁnity mi-
crobubble agents using our customized experimental platform.
From here, we delivered Cas9-sgRNA RNP complexes targeting
the EGFP (enhanced green ﬂuorescent protein) gene to EGFP-
expressing hiPSCs for EGFP knockout. Simultaneous acoustic
cavitation detection during treatment con ﬁrmed a strong cor-
relation between microbubble disruption and viable FUS-
mediated protein delivery in hiPSCs. This study shows for
the ﬁrst time the potential for an FUS-mediated technique
for targeted and precise CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing in human
stem cells.
INTRODUCTION
CRISPR-Cas9 systems have quickly emerged at the forefront of gene
editing and can be used to efﬁciently edit target gene sequences within
the genome.1,2 As a result, CRISPR editing systems have been heavily
considered for the treatment of genetic diseases (e.g., familial cardio-
myopathy)
3,4; however, a lack of translatable delivery strategies has
hindered their use for in vivo applications. Nevertheless, Cas9 is being
employed across multiple ongoing clinical trials, mainly in an ex vivo
context. Indeed, the ﬁrst CRISPR-based medicine sold under the
brand name CASGEVY was recently approved by the US Food and
Drug Administration for the treatment of sickle cell disease and trans-
fusion-dependent b-thalassemia.
5
CRISPR systems can be used to precisely edit genes through different
mechanisms (e.g., homology-directed repair), but for the purpose of
simple gene loss of function (LOF), non-homologous end joining
(NHEJ) is generally employed. 6 CRISPR-associated protein 9
(Cas9) in complex with a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) works by ﬁrst
generating a double-stranded break (DSB) at a target site speci ﬁed
by the sgRNA. NHEJ occurs as a result of imperfect endogenous
DNA repair mechanisms leading to the formation of short indels
(insertions or deletions) at the targeted location, which can cause
frameshift mutations in protein-coding regions. These frameshift
mutations may lead to early gene termination (e.g., by generating
an early stop codon), leading to an LOF of the targeted gene.
7
Cas9 is perhaps most ef ﬁciently delivered to human cells with an
sgRNA as a ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex. This delivery format
has been shown to provide high targeting ef ﬁciency,
8,9 provide quick
DNA cleavage post-transfection, 10 and lack insertional mutagenesis
relative to other strategies. 11 Current delivery methods for Cas9-
sgRNA RNPs include microinjection, electroporation, liposomes,
lipid nanoparticles, and viral delivery methods.
12–14 Microinjection
delivers Cas9 RNPs directly to the cell nucleus and is highly ef ﬁcient;
however, it provides low-throughput and is limited to in vitro trans-
fection.15,16 Electroporation is commonly used for high-throughput
gene editing and ef ﬁciently delivers Cas9 RNPs using pulsed high-
voltage current across cell surfaces, but like other physical transfec-
tion methods, is generally limited for in vitro and ex vivo applications
due to low tissue penetration depth and high toxicity. 14,17 The use of
liposomes and lipid nanoparticles is being explored for in vivo appli-
cations, but this poses the risk of unwanted immunogenic response
and suffers from high levels of endosomal entrapment, hindering
the ef ﬁciency of gene editing.
18,19 Recently developed viral delivery
methods such as the use of lentiviral capsid particles 12,13 show prom-
ise but are viral in nature and can lead to unwanted immunogenic
response. Adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) show perhaps the highest

RESULTS
Delivery of 150-kDa FITC-dextran into hiPSCs in suspension
Cas9 RNPs are highly ef ﬁcient and have demonstrated low fre-
quencies of off-target editing within the cells; however, their large
size (/C24160 kDa) and negatively charged surface make them relatively
impermeable to cells. To con ﬁrm their inability to readily enter cells,
we incubated hiPSCs with increasing concentrations of EGFP-target-
ing Cas9-sgRNA RNPs and measured the frequency of EGFP loss
8 days post-incubation. We observed no signi ﬁcant difference in
the percentage of EGFP-expressing cells across Cas9 RNP concentra-
tions ( Figure 1 A). For physical transfection methods such as FUS-
mediated delivery, the large cargo size is perhaps the greatest hurdle
to overcome as it requires the US-induced surface pores to be at least
as large as the diameter of the Cas9 protein. Despite evidence of small-
cargo delivery (e.g., plasmid, small interfering RNA, microRNA), a
limited literature exists on FUS and microbubble-mediated delivery
of large macromolecules such as Cas9 RNPs. Therefore, as a surrogate
for Cas9 RNP transfection, we ﬁrst delivered a 150-kDa ﬂuorescein
isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran protein to optimize US parameters,
both to increase throughput and determine whether hiPSCs could

36 The microbubble dose was halved to 125 microbubbles per

passive entry across the membrane, delivery ranged from 6.24% ±
0.52% to 47.38% ± 1.17% at 208 and 416 kPa, respectively (Figure 3A).
As the main objective of the surrogate experiment was to optimize
protein delivery while maintaining high cell viability, the PNP of
364 kPa was chosen for subsequent surrogate deliveries to further
optimize parameters for FUS and microbubble-induced Cas9 RNP
transfection. Between the two highest PNPs, the non-signi ﬁcant
change in sham-normalized FITC-dextran delivery with a signi ﬁcant
decrease in cell viability ( Figure 3 B) led to this decision. The treat-
ment at 364 kPa yielded a high sham-normalized delivery of
42.24% ± 1.89% while maintaining a relatively high cell viability of
94.54% ± 0.80%.

FUS-induced Cas9 RNP transfection
Following the surrogate experiments, we next delivered Cas9:sgRNA
RNP complexes (1:3 M ratio) at 6.7 /C210/C01 pmol/mL at a 1-MHz fre-
quency, 1,000 burst cycles, 5-ms pulse intervals to EGFP expressing
317-12-Ff hiPSCs ( Figure 1 C) in suspension with microbubbles at
125 microbubbles per cell (1.5 /C210
6 cells). We used Cas9-sgRNA
RNPs targeting EGFP ( Figure 1 D) to induce indels, using loss of
EGFP ﬂuorescence as a readout. Editing ef ﬁciency was measured
8 days post-transfection with ﬂow cytometry using non-EGFP-ex-
pressing hiPSC 201B7 as a positive control for the EGFP LOF.
EGFP LOF was measured at 10.57% ± 0.16% when normalized to
sham (here, non-treated EGFP-expressing hiPSCs) to control for
non-EGFP-expressing cells and random loss of ﬂuorescence within
the population (Figures 5A–5D). A cell viability assay was performed
immediately after Cas9 transfection with an observable and signi ﬁ-
cant decrease in cell viability in the treated sample at 77.18% ±
0.60% relative to sham ( Figure 5 D). To account for the change in
cell viability post-transfection, we also performed a 72-h 3-(4,5-
dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT)
viability assay, which demonstrated a drop in cell viability in treated
cells to 64.87% ± 0.82% (Figure 5C). These decreases in cell viability
are notable; however, they may be due to the cytotoxic effects of Cas9
DSBs, an issue not previously seen with FITC-dextran delivery due to
its inert nature.
DISCUSSION
In this study, we demonstrated for the ﬁrst time that FUS and micro-
bubbles can be used to deliver Cas9 RNPs to hiPSCs, undifferentiated
human stem cells with the ability to differentiate into a variety of cell
types (e.g., cardiomyocytes), as proof of concept for FUS-mediated
CRISPR gene editing therapy with RNPs. We show FUS-mediated de-
livery of large protein surrogates (150 kDa) similar in size to
Cas9 RNPs into hiPSCs, with upward of 42.24% ± 1.89% delivery
while maintaining high cell viabilities of 94.54% ± 0.80%

precise window without inducing a DSB. Base editing involves the use
of catalytically impaired Cas9 proteins with inactivated endonuclease
domains (e.g., nCas9) fused to deaminase proteins (e.g., adenine de-

the fused deaminase enzymes to catalyze 4 of the 12 possible base

51 The lack of endonuclease activity and fused base editor cir-
cumvents the requirement for a DSB to induce a genomic edit along
with the associated cytotoxicity. Newer and more ef ﬁcient precision
editing tools such as CRISPR-based prime editors utilize an inacti-
vated Cas9 protein fused to a reverse transcriptase (RT) enzyme,
and an extended gRNA called prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA)
to cause genomic edits without the requirement of a DSB. The fused
RT allows for the extended RNA domain of the pegRNA RT to be
copied into the targeted DNA sequence. Prime editors can in turn
mediate all 12 base-base conversions as well as targeted indels in a
genomic sequence.
52 However, their large size has limited delivery
as an RNP — perhaps a limitation that could be overcome with FUS
and microbubbles. Nevertheless, the high ef ﬁciency and low cytotox-
icity of prime editors offers a highly ef ﬁcient alternative and already
has been established is a variety of hard-to-transfect human cell lines
such as primary human T cells

treatment of cardiac genetic disorders such as hypertrophic cardio-
myopathy and dilated cardiomyopathy. Due to a limited number of
alternative treatment strategies, its use remains pre-clinical because
of concerns surrounding potential in -vivo viable delivery methods.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Cell culture
The 201B7 hiPSCs 56 were obtained from American Type Culture
Collection. The 317-12-Ff hiPSCs ( Figure 1 C) were generously pro-
vided by Dr. Knut Woltjen from the Center of iPS Cell Research
and Application (CiRA).
57 hiPSCs were cultured in 6-well plates ma-
trix coated in either Corning Matrigel or iMatrix-511 Silk (Nippi) as
per the manufacturer ’s protocol in mTeSR Plus or eTeSR media
(STEMCELL Technologies). Cells were kept at 37
/C14C, 5% CO 2, and
passaged every 3 –4 days at 80% –90% con ﬂuency by dissociating
them with Accutase (STEMCELL Technologies) according to the
manufacturer’s protocol. After dissociation, cells were resuspended
and /C24100,000 cells were transferred into freshly coated plates, with
2 mL mTeSR Plus or eTeSR media containing 10 mM Y-27632
ROCK inhibitor (STEMCELL Technologies). After 24 h, old media
was replaced with 2 mL fresh media and replaced every 1–2 days until
the following passage.
Ultrasound apparatus and setup
A custom-focused US treatment tank was employed for all transfec-
tions. The treatment tank consisted of a 30-L plastic container with
two co-aligned single-element transducers and an acrylic treatment
cuvette centered between the transducers. Before experiments, the
tank was ﬁlled with /C2415 L gas-equilibrated distilled H
2O, enough
to cover both transducers, but kept below the opening of the treat-
ment cuvette to reduce the risk of sample contamination. The water
temperature was maintained at 37 /C14C using an immersion heat circu-
lator (VWR model 1120, VWR International). A magnetic stir bar
was placed inside the treatment cuvette sealed with Mylar windows
(25 mm thickness) to allow ultrasonic transmissions. The treatment
cuvette was placed above a magnetic stir plate to allow samples to
mix throughout treatment ( Figure 1B).
FUS and microbubble-mediated FITC-dextran delivery
The 201B7 cells were resuspended in Accutase (STEMCELL Technol-
ogies) per the manufacturer ’s protocol. Cells were centrifuged at
500 rpm for 5 min at room temperature and resuspended in 5 mL
eTeSR media for counting. We added 20 mL cell suspension to
20 mL trypan blue (Gibco) for dead cell exclusion and counted with
Invitrogen Countess 3 Automated Cell Counter. We suspended
1.5 /C210
6 cells in a total volume of 3 mL eTeSR media per sample
and kept at room temperature until treatment. Microbubble vials
were prepared by incubating at room temperature for 15 min before
shaking in a vial shaker for 45 s, per the manufacturer ’s instructions.
Microbubbles were allowed to cool at room temperature for 15 min
after activation before being inverted for 2 min and extracted using
a 19G PrecisionGlide syringe needle (no. 305196, BD Biosciences).
58
For all experiments, De ﬁnity microbubbles were used within 1 day of
activation. A 25-mg/mL 150-kDa FITC-dextran solution in eTeSR
was used as stock and added to cells with microbubbles in the treat-
ment cuvette. The cell mixture was allowed to stir for 30 s prior to
treatment, allowing for a homogeneous distribution of microbubbles
and cells. Samples were then treated with FUS using treatment trans-
ducer (f = 25.4 mm, f# = 1.33) at 1 MHz with 1,000 cycles and 5-ms
pulse repetition intervals (duty cycle of 20%). PNPs ranging from 208
to 416 kPa were generated from an arbitrary function generator
(AFG31000, Tektronix) and ampli ﬁed using an RF power ampli ﬁer
(model no. 105A100B, Ampli ﬁer Research). Acoustic pressure was
calibrated in a separate water tank using a hydrophone (ONDA).
Post-treatment, cells were allowed to recover at room temperature
for 5 min before being put on ice until washing.

A Cas9:sgRNA RNP complex was assembled per the manufacturer ’s
protocol. Brieﬂy, for each FUS-treated sample, 6.0 nmol of lyophilized
synthetic sgRNA was spun down and resuspended in 60 mL1 /C2TE
(Tris-EDTA) buffer, pH 8.0. We added 140 mL nuclease-free water

PI cell viability assay
Immediately after FUS treatment, a subset of the treated cells was al-
lowed to recover for 5 min before being put on ice until centrifuga-
tion. Cells were then centrifuged at 500 rpm for 5 min at 4
/C14C and
washed twice in eTeSR media and once in PBS (without calcium
and magnesium; Wisent) before being resuspended in 1.5 mL PBS
and passed through a 35-mm cell strainer to remove large cell clumps.
Cells were kept on ice until ﬂow cytometry was performed. A total of
1.5 mLP I( ﬁnal concentration of 1 mg/mL) was added to each sample,
vortexed, and incubated for 1 min before being analyzed with ﬂow cy-
tometry. All ﬂow readouts were performed using an Attune CytPix
(Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc). For FITC-dextran surrogate transfections
to 201B7 cells, voltages to gate for FITC and PI were set using a nega-
tive control or sham control for each experiment. FITC
+/PI/C0cells
were gated and normalized to sham control. PI + cells were used to
determine cell death associated with each FUS condition. For FUS
transfection of Cas9 to 317-12-Ff cells for EGFP LOF, voltage to
gate for PI was set using sham control with and without PI. Flow cy-
tometry plots and images were analyzed using Attune NxT software
and edited in Inkscape and Fiji-ImageJ, respectively.
MTT cell viability assay
A 4-h MTT cell proliferation assay was used to measure cell
viability 72 h post-Cas9 RNP transfection. A subset of cells post-trans-
fection was washed twice in eTeSR and seeded at a density of
5,000 cells/well of an iMatrix-511-coated 96-well plate in triplicate
and maintained using the above cell culture protocol until assay. At
72 h post-seeding, old media was removed and washed three times
with PBS. A 100- mL 1.2-mM MTT eTeSR solution was added to each
well and incubated at 37
/C14C for 4 h. After 4 h, 25mL MTT supplemented
media was removed from each well. We added 50 mL DMSO to each
well and allowed them to incubate for 10 min at 37 /C14C. After incuba-
tion, absorbance at 540 nm was measured using a Varioskan LUX mi-
croplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scienti ﬁc) and analyzed to determine
cell viability.
EGFP LOF assay
To test for FUS-mediated transfection of hiPSCs, an EGFP LOF assay
was utilized. We used 317-12-Ff cells expressing EGFP at the AAVS1
locus for the assay. Post-transfection with Cas9:sgRNA RNP, cells
were allowed to recover 5 min at room temperature before being
placed on ice until centrifugation. Cells were centrifuged at
500 rpm for 5 min at 4
/C14C and washed twice in eTeSR. Approximately
1.0 /C2105 cells were concentration matched and seeded on iMatrix-
511 (Nippi)-coated 6-well plates in eTeSR media supplemented
with 10 mM Y-27632 ROCK inhibitor and allowed to recover for
4 days. After 4 days, the cells were resuspended using Accutase and
passed on to fresh iMatrix-511-coated 6-well plates in duplicate for
day 8 ﬂow cytometry and microscopy. Day 8 cells were resuspended
using Accutase and centrifuged at 500 rpm. Following centrifugation,
cells were resuspended in 1.5 mL PBS for ﬂow analysis. For ﬂow cy-
tometry, the Attune CytPix was used to measure the change in EGFP
expression across samples. Voltages to gate for EGFP were set using a
1:1 201B7:317-12-Ff cell solution. EGFP cells were gated and normal-
ized to sham control. Flow cytometry plots were analyzed using
Attune NxT software and edited in Inkscape.
Microscopy
All cell images were taken with an EVOS M7000 inverted ﬂuores-
cence microscope (Invitrogen). Separate plates of iMatrix-511-coated
6-well plates were imaged 8 days post-transfection. Cells were washed
once in PBS before being incubated with a 1:1,000 dilution of Hoechst
nuclear stain in 1 mL eTeSR media. Before imaging, cells were washed
three times in PBS and imaged in eTeSR media. During ﬂow cytom-
etry, single-cell images were captured with Attune CytPix imaging
software to ensure there was no signi ﬁcant change in cell size or
morphology.
Statistical analysis and Figures
All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version
10.3.1. For FITC-dextran experiments, a one-way ANOVA followed
by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to determine signi ﬁcance between
samples. All experiments were conducted in duplicate ( n = 2). A two-
way Student’s t test was used to determine signiﬁcance across samples
for EGFP loss of ﬂuorescence (n = 2). The graphs were generated us-
ing GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1.
